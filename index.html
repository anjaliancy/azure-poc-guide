Building a proof of concept on Azure

    From idea to a working proof of concept on Azure

    A guide for people who know their subject well and want to test an idea with a real, working system, without committing to a full project first.

    How to read this guide

    Everything is explained twice. The plain-language layer needs no technical background. Switch to With technical detail (top of the page) and extra blue panels appear with settings, commands and checks for the person who will actually build it. Your choice is remembered, and the print button saves whichever view you have open.

    Running example Throughout, we follow a made-up company building LeaveLoop, a simple tool for requesting and approving time off. Employees submit requests, managers approve or decline, and HR sees a summary. It is fictional, so nothing real or confidential appears anywhere in this guide.

    The journey in seven steps

    1. Frame the questionWhat must this proof of concept (POC) show? (Part 1)
    2. Listen and designTalk to the people involved, find the real problem, break it into parts. (Part 2)
    3. Choose and costCompare the options honestly and price the one you pick. (Part 3)
    4. Get accessAsk for the permissions early, because waiting is the usual delay. (Parts 4 and 5)
    5. Build a thin sliceOne complete path from start to finish, before anything else. (Part 6)
    6. Put it on AzureFirst a quick demo, then the properly secured version. (Parts 7 and 8)
    7. Show it and decideMeasure against the question you started with. (Part 9)

    One-page cheat sheet

    Reference architecture: Static Web App (front end) → App Service (API, Python/Node/.NET) → Azure SQL (data), with Key Vault (secrets), Blob Storage (files), a managed identity (the API's password-less login) and Application Insights (monitoring).

az group create -n $RG -l $LOC --tags CostCenter=<code>

az staticwebapp create -g $RG -n $SWA -l <region> --sku Free

az webapp config appsettings set -g $RG -n $APP --settings ENABLE_ORYX_BUILD=true SCM_DO_BUILD_DURING_DEPLOYMENT=true

# front end: build with the API address, then upload ./dist with the Static Web Apps CLI

    Five traps: missing ENABLE_ORYX_BUILD (503), zip with Windows paths/line endings, CORS origin mismatch, a front-end API address baked in at build time, and role assignments you are not permitted to create.

    Part 1

    What a proof of concept is, and is not

    A POC answers one question: is this idea worth investing in? It is not a small version of the finished product.

    POC

    Short, focused, fake or non-sensitive data. Built to be judged, then kept, changed or thrown away.

    Pilot

    Real users, real data, limited scope, support in place. Only after a POC says yes.

    Production

    Everyone, full security, backups, monitoring, an owner on call. A project, not an experiment.

    Six things to write down before any work starts

    DecideWhat good looks likeLeaveLoop
    The questionOne sentence a sponsor can say yes or no to"Can employees and managers handle leave online faster, with a clear history of who approved what?"
    Users and rolesThree to five roles, a handful of real peopleEmployee, manager, HR viewer
    In and out of scopeA written "not now" listIn: request, approve, summary. Out: payroll link, mobile app, public holidays by country
    Success measuresNumbers or observations you can checkRequest-to-decision time; 4 of 5 testers say they would switch
    Time box2 to 4 weeks of building, then a decision3 weeks + 1 week to test
    DataFake or non-sensitive only, unless approved otherwiseInvented employees and requests
    The rule that keeps a POC small If a feature does not help answer the question, it is out of scope. Write it on the "not now" list so nobody feels ignored.
    Part 2

    Ideation: from a vague problem to a clear plan

    Most failed POCs solve the wrong problem. This part is about finding the right one, together with the people who live with it, and then cutting it into pieces small enough to build.

    2.1 Who to talk to

    Before the first conversation, list everyone who touches the process. Four groups matter most:

    GroupWhy they matterLeaveLoopTime
    The people who do the workThey know the real steps, not the official onesHR administrators who process requests30 min each
    The people who use the outputThey show what is actually neededManagers planning team cover30 min each
    The owner or sponsorDefines success and constraintsHead of HR45 min
    Risk, security and ITThey can stop you late if you skip them earlyData protection officer, cloud team30 min

    Aim for 6 to 10 conversations. After about five on the same theme, you mostly hear repeats.

    2.2 How to run a good conversation

    • Ask about the last time, not in general. "Walk me through the last time you approved leave" gives facts; "How do you usually approve leave?" gives a tidy story.
    • Listen for workarounds. A personal spreadsheet or a sticky note is a requirement nobody wrote down.
    • Ask to see the real files. Looking at the actual form teaches more than a description of it.
    • Do not pitch your solution. People then answer politely instead of honestly.
    • Ask "what happens then?" and "why?" two or three times. Stay curious, not corrective.
    • Two people, one speaking. One asks, the other writes down exact words.
    • Play it back. Send a short summary within a day and ask "what did I get wrong?"

    Question bank

    1. Walk me through the last time you did this, from the first trigger to the last step.
    2. Which step takes the longest? Which one do you dread?
    3. Where do mistakes or delays usually happen? What did the last one cost?
    4. What do you keep outside the official system (notes, personal files)? Why?
    5. What do you check before you trust a number or a form?
    6. If you could change one thing tomorrow, what would it be?
    1. What decision do you make with this information? When do you need it?
    2. What do you do today when it is late or looks wrong?
    3. Who do you ask when you do not understand it?
    4. What would make you trust it more?
    5. What would you never want shown to others?
    1. What would make you say "this was worth it" in six months?
    2. What is the cost of the problem today, in time, money, risk or reputation?
    3. What must not change? What is open to change?
    4. Who else must be on board for this to work?
    5. What would make you stop this effort?
    1. What data classes may a POC hold, and where may they be stored?
    2. Which regions, naming rules and tags are mandatory?
    3. Which sign-in method must we use?
    4. What reviews or approvals are needed before real users try it?
    5. Who owns the system after the POC if it succeeds?

    2.3 Making sense of what you heard

    1. Find the real problem with "five whys". Ask why repeatedly until you reach a cause you could act on.
      LeaveLoopRequests are approved late. Why? Managers do not see them. Why? They arrive by email among hundreds. Why? There is no single place that shows what awaits a decision. So the problem is visibility, not approval rules.
    2. Draw the journey as it happens today: one row per step, with who does it, what hurts, and the evidence (a quote, a number, a file).
    3. Turn each pain into a "How might we…" question. "How might we show managers what is waiting for them?" This keeps options open.
    4. Write the problem in one sentence and test it with two interviewees. If they do not nod, keep going.

    2.4 Design thinking in five minutes

    Design thinking is a way of working that starts with people and tests ideas early and cheaply. It has five stages, and you move back and forth between them.

    1 · Empathise

    Understand people by listening and observing. (Part 2.1 to 2.2)

    2 · Define

    State the problem in one clear sentence. (2.3)

    3 · Ideate

    Generate many options before choosing one. Quantity first, judgement later.

    4 · Prototype

    Make a cheap version people can react to. (2.6)

    5 · Test

    Watch real people use it; learn; adjust.

    Repeat

    The first answer is rarely the final one. Plan for two rounds.

    The double diamond

    A useful picture of the same idea: open up, then narrow down, twice. First to find the right problem, then to find the right solution.

    A useful question: what job is it hired to do?

    "Jobs to be done" asks why someone would use a tool, not what features it has. Managers do not want a leave system; they want to plan team cover without chasing people. The job shapes the priorities.

    2.5 Breaking the idea into parts

    Roles and what each may do

    RoleCan seeCan change
    EmployeeOwn requestsCreate and withdraw own requests
    ManagerTheir team's requestsApprove or decline
    HR viewerEverything, as summariesNothing

    The workflow as states

    Draft→Submitted→ApprovedorDeclined

    For each arrow, write who may make the move. For each state, write who may change what. These two small tables become the rule book for the whole system.

    User stories

    One line per need: As a [role], I want [action], so that [benefit]. "As a manager, I want to see waiting requests in one list, so that I can plan cover."

    Must, should, could, won't (this time)

    Must
    • Employee submits a request
    • Manager approves or declines
    • History of who did what and when
    Should
    • Email reminder to the manager
    • HR summary by month
    Could
    • Calendar view of the team
    Won't (this time)
    • Payroll link, mobile app, holiday rules by country

    The thin slice

    Choose the smallest path that goes all the way through: an employee submits, a manager approves, HR sees it. Build that completely before adding anything. It proves the idea, exposes the real obstacles early, and always leaves you with something to show.

    2.6 Cheap prototypes, early feedback

    • Paper or slides. Sketch the main screens. Walk someone through them.
    • Clickable mock-up. Linked slides or a design tool. Good for layout and wording.
    • "Wizard of Oz". The screen looks real; a person does the work behind it. Tests whether the idea helps before you build the engine.
    • Working thin slice. The real thing, minimal, with fake data.

    Test with three to five people. Ask them to complete a task and stay quiet. Where they hesitate is where the design needs work. Write each finding down with the person's own words.

    2.7 Working with the team

    • A one-page brief everyone has seen. Template below. Update it when scope moves.
    • A weekly 20-minute show-and-tell. Show what works; collect reactions; no slides.
    • A decision log. Date, decision, why, who. It ends arguments about what was agreed.
    • Say "that's for later" kindly. "Great idea; it's on the next-version list, and here's how we will judge when to do it."
    • Agree who decides what. Who accepts scope changes? Who signs off the demo?
    • Share what you hear. People contribute more when they see their input used.

    Technical detail

    From the brief to a data model

    Turn the nouns into tables and the states into a rule table. For LeaveLoop: employees (role, manager), leave_requests (employee, dates, status, decided_by, decided_at, reason), audit_log (who, what, old value, new value, when).

    • Key by the right thing. If a status is stored "per employee", next year's request overwrites this year's. Key by what makes a record unique, usually unit and period.
    • Constraints in the database (CHECK ranges, UNIQUE, foreign keys) catch mistakes the code misses.
    • Write the rule table first: (from_state, to_state) → roles allowed and state → role → writable fields. Enforce it on the server.
    • Review the schema with a human before any screen is built. It is the most expensive thing to change later.
    Part 3

    Choosing the approach, and what it will cost

    Before building on Azure, check that building is the right answer. Then choose the simplest design that answers the POC question, and price it honestly.

    3.1 The options on the table

    OptionGood whenWatch out for
    Improve what you have (better spreadsheet, shared form)The pain is mostly habit or clarityHits a ceiling on history, permissions and scale
    Buy a productA standard tool already fits 80 percentLicence cost per user; hard to bend to your process
    Low-code platform (forms and flows)Simple process, quick need, citizen developersLimits on rules, data and cost at scale
    Build on AzureYour process is specific; you need control of data, rules and historyNeeds a builder and an owner; you maintain it
    Do nothing (for now)The problem is small or the timing is wrongThe cost of waiting; make it a conscious choice

    3.3 What a POC really costs

    The biggest cost is usually people A few weeks of a developer's and a subject lead's time typically cost many times more than the cloud bill for the same period. Include it, or the comparison is misleading.

    What drives the Azure bill

    • Size of the hosting plan. You rent capacity whether anyone is using it or not.
    • Database size. Also billed while idle.
    • Extras for security and resilience. Firewalls, private networking and geographic backups are valuable and can cost more than the app itself.
    • How many environments you run (demo, test, production).
    • Forgotten resources. Experiments left running are the most common surprise.

    Free or very cheap tiers cover a POC well: a free static site, a small basic web plan, a small database. Plan to pay noticeably more only when real users and real data arrive.

    Rough monthly cost estimator

    Rounded planning figures in US dollars, to compare designs, not a quote. Prices vary by region, change over time and depend on usage. Confirm in the Azure pricing calculator before you commit.

    3.4 Choosing between Azure alternatives

    The principle: choose the simplest service that answers the question, and leave the rest for later. You can move up when you have evidence.

    DecisionSimplest choiceMove up when
    Where the screens liveStatic Web Apps (free)You need server-rendered pages or a gateway in front
    Where the logic runsApp Service (one small plan)Containers (Container Apps) for many services; Functions for event-driven tasks
    Where the data livesDemo: a file database on the web app. POC with real-ish data: Azure SQLPostgreSQL if your team standardises on it; Cosmos DB for very flexible or global data
    Where files goBlob Storage (private)Immutable storage when records must not change
    How people sign inA simple login (demo only)Microsoft Entra ID before real data

    3.5 Using AI to help you choose

    AI assistants are good thinking partners for this part of the work. Use them to widen and sharpen your thinking, and use people and official sources to decide and confirm.

    Useful for
    • Listing options you had not considered
    • Drafting criteria and weights for you to adjust
    • Explaining a pricing page or a service in plain words
    • Building a first-pass cost model with its assumptions listed
    • Challenging your choice: "what would make this fail?"
    • Turning interview notes into themes
    Be careful about
    • Prices and limits: assistants can state them confidently and wrongly. Always confirm in the pricing calculator or with IT.
    • Confidential data: do not paste personal or sensitive information; use the invented example.
    • Out-of-date knowledge: products change.
    • Authority: the assistant suggests; your team decides and owns it.

with a suggested weight from 1 to 5 and a one-line definition of what a score of 1, 3 and 5 means.

    A good habit Keep a short note of what the AI suggested, what you verified, and what you changed. It shows your reasoning to the sponsor and protects you if a figure turns out to be off.

    3.6 Guardrails against surprise costs

    • Set a budget alert on day one at 50, 80 and 100 percent.
    • Tag every resource with an owner and cost centre.
    • Put a review date in the diary to shut down or scale down.
    • Keep one named person responsible for the bill.
    Technical detail

    Pricing and budgets

    • Use the Azure pricing calculator and pick the correct region. Check hours-per-month assumptions (730 for always-on).
    • Budget alert and tag: az consumption budget or Cost Management in the portal; tag at creation with --tags CostCenter=... Owner=....
    • Cost drivers in order of size for this stack: App Service plan, SQL database, any edge/WAF or private networking, then monitoring ingestion. Storage and Key Vault are small at POC volumes.
    • Log Analytics bills by data ingested; keep retention short for a POC.

    Part 5

    Getting access, and why to ask early

    The most common reason a POC slips is not the building. It is waiting for permission.

    Inside an organisation's cloud, three levels of permission matter, and the person building usually has only the first:

    1 · Create things

    Make the web app, the database and the storage. Usually granted for one project folder.

    2 · Let things talk to each other safely

    Allow the application to open the safe and the filing room without a password. Needs a higher permission that IT guards.

    3 · Organisation-wide settings

    Switching on services for the whole subscription. Held by very few people.

    A POC can proceed with level 1 alone, using simpler settings and fake data. Levels 2 and 3 are needed before anything real.

    Send these questions to IT on the first day

    Copy, paste, and send. Do not wait until you need the answers.

    Hello,

We are starting a short proof of concept [one-line description] using fake data only.
Could you help with the following?

1. Which subscription and resource group should we use, and which regions are approved?
2. Are there mandatory tags (for example cost centre and owner) or a naming standard?
3. Are Azure Static Web Apps allowed, or must the front end sit behind another gateway?
4. Can I be given permission to create role assignments, or who can create them for us?
5. Are private networking or a web application firewall required even for a POC?
6. Which sign-in method must we use if real users are involved?
7. Are there limits on what data a POC may hold?
8. Is my access time-limited (activated per session)? If so, how do I activate it?

Our target is to have a demo ready by [date]. Many thanks.
    Allow for time-limited access Some organisations grant powerful roles only for a few hours at a time, activated on request. If something that worked in the morning is denied in the afternoon, the role has probably expired; activate it again.

    Part 6

    Building it: the order that works

    Build foundations first. Each step ends with something a non-technical colleague can review.

    1. The recordsDecide what is stored and how records relate. Review it with the subject lead. This is the hardest thing to change later.
    2. The rule bookWrite who may do what, in which state. Put the rules in one place the whole system obeys.
    3. The historyEvery change is recorded: who, what, before, after, when. Nothing is silently overwritten.
    4. Who is whoA simple sign-in for the POC, shaped so a proper one can replace it later.
    5. The main journeyThe thin slice from Part 2, then other roles.
    6. Safety netsAutomatic tests for the rules that would quietly corrupt data if wrong, including checks that forbidden actions are refused.
    7. Demo dataAn invented dataset that covers every state, so any reviewer sees every screen in a minute.
    LeaveLoop Records: employees, requests. Rules: only a manager can approve, only their own team, only a submitted request. History: "Priya approved Sam's request on 4 March". Tests: an employee cannot approve their own request. Demo data: requests in every state.
    Why rules must live on the server Hiding a button is courtesy, not security. Anyone can send a request to the system directly, so the system itself must refuse what is not allowed.

    Deploy an empty skeleton in week one. Problems with permissions, regions and policy show up early, when the app is empty, not the night before the demo.

    Technical detail
    1. Schema first (SQLite locally, Azure SQL in the cloud). Plain SQL in a portable subset (? placeholders, no ON CONFLICT/MERGE); one small file that knows the dialect differences; a test that fails if the two schema files drift.
    2. Rule table: TRANSITIONS: (from, to) → roles and EDITABLE: state → role → fields, validated in the API for every write. Return 403 for role problems and 409 for state problems.
    3. Audit: one row per changed field, written in the same transaction as the change; if nothing changed, write nothing. Append-only (ledger table on Azure SQL).
    4. Auth: signed, expiring tokens; keep current_user and require_roles as the only things routes depend on, so Entra ID can replace the login.
    5. Tests: rules, permissions (including the must-be-refused cases), scoring or calculations. Run on every change.
    6. Seed script: idempotent, gated by a setting (SEED_DEMO_DATA), only on an empty database.
    7. Operations: /health and /health/db from day one; configuration in environment variables; a single entry point for database access.
    Part 7

    Putting it on Azure, in two stages

    First get something clickable quickly. Then make it properly secure. Do not skip the first stage, and do not mistake it for the second.

    Stage 1 · Demo

    Goal: a public link people can click this week.

    • Fake data only
    • Simple shared login
    • Data held in a simple file on the web app
    • Secrets stored as settings

    Good for: feedback sessions and the first demo.

    Stage 2 · Proper

    Goal: safe enough to trust with real information.

    • A managed database with change history
    • The application signs in with its own identity, no passwords
    • Secrets in a vault
    • Monitoring and a tested backup

    Needed before: any real data or real users.

    What happens in Stage 1, in plain terms: create a project folder; rent a small server for the back office; create a free place for the screens; give the back office its settings; upload the code; build the screens pointing at the back office; upload them; check that the health page says OK; try each role.

    What changes in Stage 2: a database is created, an administrator sets up its tables once, the application is given permission to read and write rows (but not to change the structure), secrets move to the vault, and the temporary demo shortcuts are removed.

    Technical detail · Stage 1

    Variables

    $RG   = "rg-leaveloop-poc"
$LOC  = "<approved region>"
$PLAN = "plan-leaveloop-poc"
$APP  = "app-leaveloop-poc-<unique>"    # becomes <APP>.azurewebsites.net, globally unique
$SWA  = "swa-leaveloop-poc"

    1 · Group and plan

    az group create -n $RG -l $LOC --tags CostCenter=<code> Owner=<you>
az appservice plan create -g $RG -n $PLAN --is-linux --sku B1

    2 · Static Web App and token

    az staticwebapp create -g $RG -n $SWA -l <swa region> --sku Free
$swaHost  = az staticwebapp show -g $RG -n $SWA --query defaultHostname -o tsv
$swaToken = az staticwebapp secrets list -g $RG -n $SWA --query properties.apiKey -o tsv

    The token is a password. Do not commit it.

    3 · The API web app

    az webapp create -g $RG -p $PLAN -n $APP --runtime "PYTHON:3.12"
az webapp config set -g $RG -n $APP --startup-file "bash startup.sh"
az webapp config appsettings set -g $RG -n $APP --settings `
  SCM_DO_BUILD_DURING_DEPLOYMENT=true ENABLE_ORYX_BUILD=true `
  "SECRET_KEY=<long random string>" "ALLOWED_ORIGINS=https://$swaHost"

    ENABLE_ORYX_BUILD=true makes App Service install your requirements during a zip deploy. Without it the site returns 503.

    4 · Deploy the API

    Build the zip with the code, requirements.txt and the startup script at the root, forward-slash paths and LF line endings (use a script with Python's zipfile, not Compress-Archive).

    az webapp deploy -g $RG -n $APP --src-path ./deploy.zip --type zip --async true
# poll https://$APP.azurewebsites.net/health  (first start installs packages: a few minutes)

    5 · Build and publish the front end

    cd frontend
$env:VITE_API_URL = "https://$APP.azurewebsites.net/api"   # baked in at build time
npm ci
npm run build
npx --yes @azure/static-web-apps-cli deploy ./dist --deployment-token $swaToken --env production

    Anything with a VITE_ (or similar) prefix is shipped to every browser: never put secrets there. Search the built bundle for the host name to confirm it points where you meant.

    6 · Verify

    • GET /health is OK; sign in as each role; try one allowed and one forbidden action.
    • Network error in the browser but the API answers in curl: check CORS (ALLOWED_ORIGINS must hold the front end's exact URL).
    az webapp log config -g $RG -n $APP --docker-container-logging filesystem
az webapp log tail   -g $RG -n $APP

    For a throwaway demo, keep the SQLite file on the web app's persistent /home disk (for example /home/data/app.db). It does not survive scaling to several instances, so move to Azure SQL as soon as the data matters.

    Technical detail · Stage 2

    Database design

    • Microsoft Entra-only authentication on the SQL server: no SQL logins exist.
    • A named person (ideally a group) is the SQL admin.
    • The API's managed identity is a database user with read and write on tables only. It cannot change the schema.
    • Optional ledger audit tables: WITH (LEDGER = ON (APPEND_ONLY = ON)). Tampering becomes detectable, not impossible; the proof is only as strong as where the digests are kept.

    One-off table setup

    Option A, from your machine (if developer machines may obtain database tokens): add your IP to the server firewall, az login --scope https://database.windows.net/.default, run your setup script as the admin, create the API's user, then remove the firewall rule.

    Option B, from inside the web app (when conditional access blocks A):

    1. Make the web app's identity the temporary SQL admin (a management call, no database sign-in): 
      az sql server ad-admin create -g $RG -s <sql server> --display-name $APP --object-id <app principal id>
    2. Run setup at the app's start, controlled by a setting such as SETUP_MODE=real: create tables, seed reference data, grant the identity read/write. If CREATE USER ... FROM EXTERNAL PROVIDER fails ("Server identity is not configured"), create the user from the identity's client id (CREATE USER [name] WITH SID = ..., TYPE = E).
    3. Watch the setup log until it reports success.
    4. Restore the human admin and delete the setup settings; restart.
    5. Confirm /health/db reports the Azure SQL database.

    Rehearse on a throwaway database whose name ends in _test, and make any destructive reset command refuse to run elsewhere.

    Connection

    Set the server and database name as app settings; use the ActiveDirectoryMSI authentication mode on Azure (ActiveDirectoryDefault locally after az login). With no server configured, fall back to SQLite so local development and tests need nothing.

    Secrets

    StageWhere secrets live
    LocalEnvironment variables or an untracked .env
    Demo, fake dataApp settings, typed by the owner, never in the repo
    Anything realKey Vault, referenced from app settings

    Reference syntax: @Microsoft.KeyVault(VaultName=<vault>;SecretName=<name>); the API's identity needs Key Vault Secrets User. Verify it resolved, otherwise the app silently uses the literal text as its secret:

    az rest --method get --url "https://management.azure.com/subscriptions/<SUB>/resourceGroups/$RG/providers/Microsoft.Web/sites/$APP/config/configreferences/appsettings?api-version=2022-03-01" --query "value[].{setting:name,status:properties.status}" -o table

    If the status is AccessToKeyVaultDenied after the role exists, re-running the same deployment (which re-saves the settings) fixes it; a plain restart may not.

    Infrastructure as code (optional but recommended)

    Describe resources in a Bicep file with one parameter file per environment; run a dry run first.

    az deployment group what-if -g $RG -f main.bicep -p poc.bicepparam   # lists changes, makes none
az deployment group create  -g $RG -f main.bicep -p poc.bicepparam
    • Generate names from a prefix, the environment and a short hash of the resource group id.
    • Apply the same tags to everything through one parameter.
    • Guard role assignments with a switch (createRoleAssignments) so the template still deploys when you may not create them.
    • Look up built-in role ids: az role definition list --name "Key Vault Secrets User" --query "[].name" -o tsv.
    • What-if cannot see quota limits; if a deployment fails on size or region, change the parameter and re-run (it is declarative).
    Part 8

    Keeping it safe

    A POC with fake data can be simple. The habits you form now decide how easily it grows up.

    Principles
    • Least access. Everyone and everything gets only what it needs.
    • No secrets in the open. Never in code, chat, slides or tickets. The person who owns a password types it in.
    • The system enforces the rules, not the screen.
    • Keep a record. Every important change says who, what and when.
    • Fake data until approved.
    What "tamper-evident" means

    A tamper-evident record is like a tamper-proof seal on a jar. It does not stop someone opening the jar, but you can tell if they did. It is a strong help to trust, and it works best alongside tight permissions about who can write at all.

    Before any real data arrives

    ItemWhy
    Proper company sign-in (and a plan for outside partners)Replace shared passwords; tie identities to roles
    Secrets in a vault, with access granted by roleNo secrets in settings
    Database not open to the public internetRemove the public path
    Edge protection and a proper address (optional, by policy)Defence at the front door
    Locked storage for change-history proofsMakes the tamper-evidence credible
    Uploaded files in private storage with limited-time linksOut of the database; versioned
    Backups across regions and a restore you have actually testedRecovery
    An owner, a runbook, and alertsSomeone is responsible
    Data classification approvedCompliance
    Technical detail

    Baseline in the POC

    • HTTPS only, TLS 1.2, FTPS disabled; health check path set.
    • Roles enforced on the server by role and workflow state; tests for the refusals.
    • Audit log in the same transaction; ledger on audit tables if using Azure SQL.
    • Managed identity for Azure-to-Azure access; no passwords in code.
    • Storage private, no shared keys; uploads limited by size and an allow-list of types (never executables or HTML).
    • Front-end build variables are public: no secrets.

    Hardening list

    • Entra ID sign-in (external tenant for partners).
    • Private endpoints or a restricted SQL firewall; remove "allow Azure services".
    • Front Door with WAF; custom domain.
    • Immutability (time-based retention) policy on the ledger digest container.
    • Blob uploads via short-lived links; geo-redundant backups; staging slot (Premium plan).
    Part 9

    Showing it, judging it, deciding

    A POC exists to be judged. Plan the demo and the decision as carefully as the build.

    The demo

    1. Start with the question, in the sponsor's words, and the success measures you agreed.
    2. Show the old way in 60 seconds, with a real example of the pain.
    3. Walk the thin slice as each role, in the order a real request travels.
    4. Show the history. This is often what wins trust.
    5. Show a refusal: someone trying something not allowed, and the system saying no.
    6. Let a real user drive.
    7. Finish with the measures, what is still unknown, and what you recommend.

    Keep a recording as a fallback in case the connection fails.

    The decision paper (one to two pages)

    • The question and the answer: yes, no, or not yet.
    • Evidence against each success measure, and what users said in their own words.
    • What it would take to run for real: people, cost range, risks, owner.
    • Options: stop, extend the POC, run a pilot, or build for production.
    • The recommendation and the decision needed, with a date.

    Monitoring and teardown

    requests   | where success == false | summarize count() by name, resultCode, bin(timestamp, 1h)

      Reference

      Glossary

      API
      The back office of an application: the part that applies the rules and talks to the data.
      Audit log
      A record of every important change: who, what, before, after, when.
      Azure
      Microsoft's cloud: rentable computing, storage and services.
      Bicep
      A text file that describes the cloud resources you want, so they can be created repeatably.
      CORS
      A browser safety rule about which web addresses may talk to the back office.
      Entra ID
      Microsoft's company sign-in service.
      Key Vault
      A safe for passwords and keys.
      Ledger
      A database feature that makes after-the-fact changes to history detectable.
      Managed identity
      A badge the application itself holds, so it needs no stored password.
      MoSCoW
      Must, Should, Could, Won't: a way to rank features.
      POC
      Proof of concept: a small, time-boxed test of whether an idea is worth pursuing.
      Resource group
      A folder that holds everything for one project in Azure.
      Role (RBAC)
      A named set of permissions that can be given to a person or application.
      SKU
      The size or tier of a service, which sets its capability and price.
      Tag
      A label on a resource, such as owner or cost centre.
      Thin slice
      The smallest complete path through the system, built first.
      WAF
      Web application firewall: filters harmful traffic before it reaches the application.
